Firearm violence across space and time in Philadelphia, PA, 2015-2025

Jamie Song, MPH for MUSA 6110, Fall 2026 | Professor Mjumbe Poe

In the modern era, firearm violence in Philadelphia has been a persistent burden on public health and safety. In the past several years, the city experienced a sharp pandemic-era increase in firearm-related injuries, followed by a decline to historically low rates, a trend that continues through the present day (2026).

What remain unclear are how this citywide trend has manifested across different parts of the city, and whether some benefited more from the decline. In this storymap project, we pose the question: how evenly has firearm injury burden declined across the census tracts of Philadelphia?

The citywide trend

Prior to the COVID-19 pandemic, firearm injuries in Philadelphia already represented a grave public health concern, with efforts to curb violence only met by steadily increasing shooting rates. Then, they surged in the wake of the 2020 lockdown, and dropped dramatically after 2022. In 2026, the city is on track to maintain its downward trend, reaching lows not seen since before firearm violence became a widespread public health issue.

Note: Counts include both fatal and nonfatal injuries.

From citywide counts to tract rates

However, only looking at citywide trends can hide variation between different parts of the city. Some areas may have benefited more from the decline than others.

In the following maps, we will use firearm injury rates per 10,000 resident population, rather than raw counts, to compare disparities across the city. We will use Census tracts to represent these areas; however, please note that tracts are areas developed and used for US Census purposes, and they are not intended to represent neighborhoods.

Please also note that, like generalizing citywide trends to smaller parts of the city, tract-level firearm injury burden is not the same as a tract resident's individual risk.

2020–2022: peak period

This map shows how firearm violence varied across the city during the post-pandemic years. Darker colors show higher annual firearm rates. Injury burden is most concentrated in areas of North Philadelphia, particularly in Kensington and along the Amtrak corridor, as well as West and Southwest Philadelphia.

Gray tracts indicate that the rate is unavailable, not that no shootings occurred. Certain tracts, such as parks, are excluded because their small populations make their rates unreliable.

2023–2025: the recent decline

This map displays rates from more recent years. We can see that the colors across tracts have lightened, which is consistent with the overall decline in firearm violence, and the widespread concentration of injuries across North, West, and Southwest Philadelphia appear to become more sparse. However, many of the previously elevated tracts still show high rates.

Note: both periods use a fixed 2020–2024 ACS population denominator, which is good for consistent comparisons but does not capture population change over time.

Where elevated burden persisted

For this map, we wanted to see which tracts had high firearm violence in 2020-2022 and/or in 2023-2025, and we wanted to make the comparison meaningful and based on prior data. So, we compared each tract in each time period against one historical benchmark: the 75th percentile of the pre-pandemic 2015–2019 annual rates, approximately 13.52 events per 10,000 population per year. Then, we categorized each tract based on whether it fell above or below this benchmark in each time period.

"Persistently elevated" tracts are those that remained above the benchmark in both time periods. "Previously elevated" tracts were above the benchmark in the earlier period but are now below it. "Newly elevated" tracts are those that are now above the benchmark but were not in the earlier period. "Consistently below" tracts are those that have remained below the benchmark in both periods. The number of tracts in each category will appear next.

Transition counts will appear after the data load.

As we can see, many tracts were previously elevated but have dropped below the benchmark, yet many also remained elevated, which is consistent with the visual patterns from the last two maps. What remains to be understood is the underlying differences between these groups, and how they compare in terms of their demographics, risk factors, and community needs.

Please note that “previously elevated” means below this benchmark in the recent period, not that violence disappeared. Also, with a fixed benchmark across both periods and widespread declines, we do not expect many newly elevated tracts.

Methods, sources, and aggregate data

2020 Census tracts; pooled three-year counts divided by fixed 2020 population × 3, multiplied by 10,000. Officer-involved shootings were excluded. Event location =/= residence. Zero or missing populations produce unavailable rates, as well as special-use exclusions.

Sources: Philadelphia shooting-victim data (extract 2026-05-21); US Census Bureau 2020 geography and 2020–2024 ACS five-year total population
